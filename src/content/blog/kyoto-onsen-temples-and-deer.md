## Prologue: Shinkansen

![bullet train with eyes illustration](/images/blog/shinkansen.webp)

We messed up when buying our Shinkansen (bullet train) tickets to go from Tokyo to Kyoto, overspending ~$80 per person by buying an extra ticket that we didn't need.

![defeated women illustration](/images/blog/sad.webp)

When buying tickets, there are signs saying that shinkansen riders need **two tickets**: a base ticket and a limited express ticket. While this is true, it is also possible to get both tickets combined in one printed ticket. This was not immediately obvious to us when using the machine, so we ended up getting a superfluous second ticket.  

Frankly, I still don't understand how to read the ticket to make sure you have the right one, so I can’t even impart any wisdom in this area.  

My advice is: If you need to buy a shinkansen ticket, and you have never bought a shinkansen ticket before, and you can’t read Japanese (or even if you can read Japanese), ***just ask a station worker for help***.\
Aside from that mishap, I had a delicious gyudon ekiben on the way to Kyoto.

![gyudon (beef over rice) train bento](/images/blog/beefrice.webp)

### Communal Bath Experience

Despite having surface-level familiarity with the rituals of a Japanese public bath, going through the rituals myself was quite intimidating.

![communal bath illustration](/images/blog/onsen.webp)

You have to be naked around other strangers who are also naked, which is not really a situation that happens in the US.  

It’s important that you get normal about this really fast, because once you get situated in the locker room, you need to strip completely naked before entering the bath area.  

Rules may vary a little for different places, but at Hotel Loisir, guests can’t take anything into the bath area except for a small towel. Everything else needs to stay in the locker, including your phone, slippers, pajamas, etc.  

The following are the order of operations of using the public bath at Hotel Loisir as I experienced it:

1. Get completely naked in the locker room and put all your stuff in the locker.
2. Enter the bath area with nothing except a small towel.
3. Go to the lightly partitioned area with showers and take a shower. (Imagine urinal partitions, except instead of urinals between the partitions, there are handheld showers and a little stool.) Shampoo, conditioner, and body wash are all provided and you need to shower and wash your body/hair as you normally do.
4. If you have long hair, tie it up. Your hair shouldn’t touch the water in the public bath.
5. Once you are clean, you can get into the large bath.
6. Sit in the bath quietly and contemplate your life. People in manga/anime are often depicted with the towel on their head like so, but personal experience shows that the towel is prone to slipping into the water.
7. Get out of the bath once you feel too hot and dry off your body with your towel.
8. Exit the bath area and get dressed and leave.

![women in onsen with towel on head. This does Not work](/images/blog/onsen-woman.webp)

Once you get used to the strict workflow and being naked around other people, it’s actually a lot of fun.

## Notable Excursions

### Toji Temple

We were deciding whether to go to Fushimi Inari, and I was already dreading the crowds while sitting in my hotel room, so my friends and I opted to go to [Toji Temple](https://maps.app.goo.gl/DhZFLDMZAHYis3G29), a five-minute walk away from our hotel, instead.

![Picture of the pagoda at Toji Temple](/images/blog/toji-temple.jpg)

This turned out to be a great decision, because the temple was serene and uncrowded, and there was a wonderful ceramic painting exhibition by artist [Kazuhisa Kusaba](https://kusaba-kazuhisa.com/en/exhibition/).

![Sign for the ceramic painting exhibition](/images/blog/tosaiga.webp)

The art was insane, with the colors of the painting changing when viewed at different angles. Photography was prohibited in the exhibition, so you’ll just have to take my word for it, or check out the website I linked above.

Admission to the exhibition was free, so if you are in the Toji Temple area before May 31st, 2026, you should check it out.  

Also look at this picture of turtles stacked on a rock in the warm sun.

![turtles stacked on each other on a rock](/images/blog/turtles.webp)

### Nara

Nara is a quick ~30 minute train ride away from Kyoto, so my friends and I decided to take a quick trip to see Nara Deer Park and Todaiji Temple.  

They were not kidding about the deer. There are deer everywhere.

![my friend feeding a dear shika senbei](/images/blog/deer.webp)

Visitors can buy special deer crackers (shika senbei) made of rice bran and flour to feed to the deer, which have learned across many, many generations to bow at visitors to solicit these crackers. I had mixed feelings about this, but I suppose this is a mutually beneficial relationship where the deer remain well-fed and visitors are entertained.  

Some of the deer get feisty, following you and nipping at your clothing even after you’ve exhausted your stack of shika senbei. My friend had his bag slobbered on, so beware!

![women being jumped by a deer illustration](/images/blog/shika_senbei_osou.webp)

It was a Saturday, so Todaiji Temple was swarming with visitors. My friends and I paid to get into the Great Buddha Hall, which was [800yen](https://www.todaiji.or.jp/en/information/haikan/) ($5 USD). The Buddha statues are massive, which should come as no surprise based on the name of the Hall.

![Todaiji Temple selfie. My friend's faces have been covered with emojis of their own choosing](/images/blog/pxl_20260411_061609996~2.webp)

There are also stamps to collect, both in the Great Buddha Hall and the neighboring Todaiji Museum. The museum stamps are in the lobby, and you do not need to pay museum admission to get them.

![Stamps from Todaiji Temple and Museum](/images/blog/stamps.webp)

### Kyoto Railway Museum

This is a fun interactive museum for train lovers. The museum itself is very child-friendly, but it can be enjoyed by train-enthusiasts of all ages. However, we visited it on a clear and sunny Sunday, so there were children everywhere.

![View of first floor of Railway museum from second floor walkway. There are various trains on display](/images/blog/trains.webp)

![Trainyard that is part of the Kyoto Railway Museum](/images/blog/trains2.webp)

My friend and I booked the driving simulator experience, which allows visitors to try out a machine that train operators use in training. I kept forgetting to brake in time and missed the station several times completely. It is probably good that I am not in this line of work.  

### Saihoji Temple

This temple is away from the main Kyoto area and only takes visitors by reservation only. It’s about 25 USD for admission and the experience.  

The experience being writing mantras in the main building, and then taking a leisurely stroll through their peaceful moss garden. Their gardens are beautiful, and I would say this was my favorite temple experience in Kyoto.

![Written mantra final result](/images/blog/mantra.webp)

![Saihoji Moss Garden](/images/blog/saihoji.webp)

### Matsuda Seihon Bookbinding

My friends and I partook in a book-making workshop here to make our own Japanese stampbooks (goshuin-cho), which I was able to book a few weeks in advance through [their website](https://kyoto-book.com/).  

The workshop and business are run by Tsutomu Matsuda-san and his daughter, Yoko Matsuda-san. Yoko-san taught the workshop in English, with the aid of her father. Tsutomu-san doesn’t speak English, but is incredibly skilled and knowledgeable.

![](/images/blog/bookbinding.webp)

![](/images/blog/bookbinding2.webp)

At the conclusion of the workshop, Yoko-san took us on a brief walk to the nearby temple to get the first goshuin for our newly created stamp book. On the way there, she told us a little about the history of the neighborhood, and how Kyoto was known for the arts.

### Arashiyama (Bamboo Forest)

There was bamboo. We walked through a nice garden to get to it. That was about it. The bamboo forest was nice, but it was crowded and more of a photo-op spot than anything else.

![](/images/blog/arashiyama.webp)

![](/images/blog/arashiyama2.webp)

There’s also a monkey park in the area, but we did not go because I do not have any strong feelings about monkeys.
