## Accommodation

My friends and I stayed at [Hotel Sails in Minowa](https://www.hotelsails.com/entop/).

![front entrance of Hotel Sails in Minowa](/images/blog/hotelsails.jpg)

Hotel Sails is located in a more residential part of Tokyo, a little further away from the city center. This meant that going to touristy destinations within the city would take anywhere from 30 minutes to an hour on the Tokyo Metro. The Metro was always reliable, so we never had any issues with it.

My friends and I chose this hotel because it offered rooms with 3 twin beds, and we were traveling as a three-person group. The room itself looked like a recovery ward, which was a bonus in our books.

![image of Hotel Sails room for 3 people](/images/blog/tripleroom.jpg "My friends couldn't stop laughing when they saw this room on the website")

They offer an equally unhinged room for 4-person groups, which you can see if you check their [website](https://www.hotelsails.com/en/rooms/).

The only thing of note was that this hotel had a strict no-guest policy, which meant that we couldn’t bring our friend in to watch Star Wars Episode 3 with us. Phooey.

## Notable Excursions

### Kanamara Penis Festival

[Kanamara Penis Festival](https://www.japan.travel/en/spot/362/) is an annual festival held at Kanayama Shrine in Kawasaki, a little out of the way from the main Tokyo area.

![Sign of goods being sold at The Kanamara Penis Festival](/images/blog/penisfestival.jpg)

It is exactly what it sounds like, and draws a large crowd of festival goers. The main shrine area has booths and vendors selling food and various phallic merch. There is a lot of fun in seeing people openly wearing penis hats and sucking on penis-shaped lollipops.

The festival includes a procession where paraders hoist three large phalluses down the street: a wooden one, an iron one, and a pink one. The pink one, named Elizabeth, has become something of a symbol of the event, and was donated by a Drag Queen Club in Tokyo.

![Image of Elizabeth being raised above the crowds](/images/blog/elizabeth.jpg)

It’s easy to make jokes about Japan being weird, but worshipers come to this shrine/festival to wish for success in marriage and childbirth, and for protection against STDs. Proceeds from the festival are used for HIV/AIDS research.

### Itoya Ginza

My friends and I spent three hours at this 12-story stationary store in Ginza. ([Itoya](https://www.ito-ya.co.jp/ext/lang/en/index.html) is a chain, and Ginza is their main store.) I bought stickers, postcards, letter paper, watercolor paper, stamps, ink pads, and several small sketchbooks. This store is a lot of fun to browse for those who are artistically inclined.

![Notebook labeled Gantt chart](/images/blog/gantt-chart.jpg)

I even found a notebook specifically for Gantt charts, for all your analog planning needs.

I was there on a Tuesday, and it was milling with people, so I can only imagine how crowded it gets on a weekend.

### Tokyo Rinkai Disaster Prevention Park

This park has a learning center with an [interactive experience/tour](https://www.tokyorinkai-koen.jp/en/1f/) that teaches visitors what to do in the 72-hours after the event of an earthquake. They load visitors up into an elevator that shakes a little and dumps them into a life-sized model of a damaged Tokyo street, complete with scary sound effects.

![Image placeholder](/images/blog/emergencyroad.jpg)

The tour goes over escaping the area and moving to a safe evacuation site following an earthquake. I learned that if you are in an elevator during an earthquake, you should mash the buttons for all the floors and get off at the first available floor. See, this is not something I ever had to think about as a New Yorker, but it makes a lot of sense.

At the conclusion of it, I felt that this would be a really funny place to take someone on a first date.

### Meguro Parasitological Museum

This quaint little museum in the Meguro area of Tokyo exhibits 300 parasite specimens, including the infamous 8.8-meter-long tapeworm that was collected from a patient in the 1980s. The museum provides a piece of string to help museum-goers visualize exactly how long that is.

![Longest preserved tapeworm in the world](/images/blog/tapeworm.jpg)

![My friends and I holding out the demonstrative string](/images/blog/tapeworm2.jpg)

### Hakone Loop (Mt Fuji)

This isn’t in Tokyo, but my friends and I did it as a day trip from Tokyo with a [guided bus tour](https://www.getyourguide.com/tokyo-l193/tokyo-mt-fuji-tour-hakone-ropeway-owakudani-lake-ashi-t1035544/). The Hakone Loop is a scenic route around the Hakone area that allows visitors to get a good view of Mount Fuji on clear days. The route includes a cable car up to the Owakudani Volcanic Valley where the air smells like sulfur and a boat ride across Lake Ashi.

![image of the boat we took across Lake Ashi](/images/blog/hakoneship.jpg)

![sulfourous valleys!](/images/blog/sulfurousvalley.jpg)

You can theoretically do it on your own, but there was a recent [Reddit post](https://www.reddit.com/r/JapanTravelTips/comments/1sgeyld/hakone_free_pass_warning/) warning tourists about the flakiness of public buses in rural Hakone, so I didn’t mind paying extra to avoid being stranded in Hakone.

The Loop is pretty popular with locals and tourists alike, so the main attractions along the loop get pretty crowded. That said, my friends and I got quite lucky with the weather, allowing us to have full visibility of Mount Fuji from various angles along the route.

![Image placeholder](/images/blog/mtfuji.jpg)

Also shout out to our tour guide Jack, who knew the best way to get around the loop and all the best spots for pictures!

## Miscellaneous Notable Mentions

* The Mega Don Quijote in Shibuya, where I learned via experience that if you plan on using an item in Japan, you should Not get it duty-free
* The Kura Sushi in Ginza, which allowed me to try the blasphemous corn-mayo sushi. I do not think Japan can complain about anything Americans do to sushi, because they do the exact same stuff.

  ![corn mayo sushi](/images/blog/cornmayo.jpg "It was delicious")
* Everyone talks about how Japan doesn't believe in public trash cans, but we should also be talking about how they don't believe in paper napkins and paper towels for drying your hands in the restrooms. The napkins are strangely waxy and there are basically no paper towels for drying your hands!
* The perception of how many people in Tokyo speak English fluently is somewhat overblown. I've had to use my scuffed Japanese in various places like the post office to buy stamps, or to ask konbini workers about stuff in stores.
* I am still unemployed!!
